## About this health information

This information is general. It is not a diagnosis and it is not medical advice for one person. Everyone's heart condition and treatment are different, so the plan from your own doctor, nurse or care team always comes first. If something here seems different from what your care team told you, follow your care team and ask them about it.

## Emergency signs: get help straight away

Call your local emergency number straight away, and do not drive yourself, if you have:

- Chest pain, pressure or tightness that lasts more than a few minutes, comes back, or spreads to the arm, neck, jaw or back.
- Sudden, severe shortness of breath, or struggling to breathe.
- Fainting or collapsing.
- Signs of a stroke: the face drooping on one side, weakness in an arm or leg, or speech that is slurred or confused.

If your doctor gave you a medicine to use for chest pain, use it as they told you. Stop any activity, sit down and stay with someone if you can.

## Shortness of breath

Feeling a little out of breath during effort can be normal, especially when you are getting fitter. It is worth noticing when it happens, what you were doing and how long it lasted.

Contact your care team soon if breathlessness is new or getting worse, comes on with less activity than before, happens when you are resting, or makes it hard to lie flat or wakes you at night. Sudden or severe breathlessness is an emergency.

## Swelling in the ankles, legs or tummy

Some heart conditions can make the body hold on to fluid. This can show as swollen ankles, legs or tummy, shoes or socks feeling tight, or weight going up quickly over a few days.

If your care team asked you to weigh yourself, do it at the same time each morning and write it down. Tell your care team if the swelling is new or getting worse, or if your weight goes up faster than they told you to watch for. Do not change how much you drink or how you take your medicines unless your care team tells you to.

## A racing, fluttering or skipping heartbeat

Many people sometimes feel their heart beat fast, flutter or skip. This is called palpitations. It can happen with stress, caffeine, poor sleep or exercise, and it is not always serious, but it is worth telling your care team about.

Note when it happens, how long it lasts and what you were doing. Contact your care team if it keeps happening. Get help straight away if it comes with chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or feeling very unwell.

## Dizziness or light-headedness

Feeling dizzy or light-headed can have many causes, including standing up quickly, not drinking enough, or some heart medicines.

If you feel dizzy, sit or lie down until it passes. Stand up slowly, especially after lying down or in the morning. Tell your care team if it happens often or started after a change in your medicines, but do not stop or change a medicine on your own. Fainting or nearly fainting needs urgent help.

## Tiredness and low energy

Feeling tired is common with a heart condition, after a hospital stay and with some medicines. Low mood and poor sleep can make it worse.

It can help to pace yourself: spread tasks through the day, rest between them, and do a little more as you feel able. Tell your care team if tiredness is new, getting worse, or stopping you from doing everyday things.

## Side effects of medicines

Heart medicines can sometimes cause side effects, such as tiredness, dizziness, a cough or upset stomach. Many settle with time, and some can be helped by your doctor or pharmacist.

Do not stop, skip or change a medicine because of a side effect without talking to your doctor or pharmacist first: stopping some heart medicines suddenly can be harmful. Write down what you notice and when it started, and ask at your next appointment, or sooner if it worries you.

## Taking medicines well

Heart medicines work best when taken regularly, as prescribed. Ideas that help:

- Take them at the same time each day, linked to a habit such as breakfast.
- Use a pill organiser or a phone reminder.
- Keep an up-to-date list of your medicines and bring it to appointments.
- If you miss a dose, check the leaflet or ask your pharmacist what to do. Do not take a double dose unless you have been told to.

Never stop or change a heart medicine without talking to your doctor or pharmacist.

## Keeping notes for your appointment

Short notes help your care team understand what is happening between visits. For each symptom, it helps to write:

- When it started and how often it happens.
- How long it lasts.
- What you were doing at the time.
- What made it better or worse.

The app's appointment questions can help you turn these notes into clear questions for your next visit.

## When to contact your care team

Contact your care team, rather than waiting for your next appointment, if:

- A symptom is new, getting worse or happening more often.
- You can do less than usual because of breathlessness, tiredness or swelling.
- You think a medicine is causing a problem.
- You are not sure whether something needs attention.

For emergency signs, call your local emergency number instead.
